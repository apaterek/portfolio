Creative Technology

Tools move fast. What a team can act on moves slowly.

My interest in creative technology has never been in the demo. It sits in the distance between a capability and a team that can actually use it on a deadline. Visual anthropology trained me to watch what people do rather than what a tool promises they will do, and that is still the useful question: what changes in the work when the tool arrives, and what does someone need to know to make that change a good one?

In media, I have written and produced inside asset-heavy editorial processes where the constraint was never the idea. It was throughput: drafts per day, archival and creative prepared in parallel, every stakeholder aligned, the result true to the comp and still original. That is precisely the environment where generative and agentic tools either earn their place or quietly do damage, and telling the difference takes having done the work by hand first.

In experiential and location-based work the same question moves into physical space, where research, concept, and production all have to agree on what a person will actually experience. Creative technology there is less about output and more about staging: what the system does, what a visitor believes it does, and where those two need to meet.

What I want to own is the internal knowledge and education infrastructure around these tools. The documentation, the training, the working examples, and the judgment calls written down where people can find them. Not hype, and not prohibition. Clear, honest guidance that Studio teams can act on, including the cases where the honest answer is that the tool is not ready yet.
