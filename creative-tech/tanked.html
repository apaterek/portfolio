TANKED A trade-grading and keeper-rebuild tool for a private NL-only fantasy baseball league, built from the valuation math up.

Austin Paterek · 08.13.26

Why I built it

In a ten-team keeper league, going out of contention is not the end of the season. It is a one-time window: convert this year's talent into next year's cheap contracts before the trade deadline closes it. Most trade tools rank players by raw value, which is exactly the wrong question for a team that is not trying to win this year's standings. The question that actually matters is whether a deal protects long-term keeper value, and whether the team on the other side, still fighting for a playoff spot, would actually say yes to it.

I write and produce inside asset-heavy pipelines for a living, so building the tool that answers that question felt like a natural place for the same instincts to land: what does the person making a decision actually need to see, and what judgment call is the software quietly making on their behalf whether they notice it or not.

What it does

SGP valuation. Converts rest-of-season projections into dollar value calibrated to this specific league's live standings spread, not a generic ranking. The three rate categories (on-base percentage, ERA, WHIP) get proper marginal handling rather than being summed like counting stats, which is where most homemade valuation math quietly breaks.

Keeper surplus. Joins that value against each player's locked salary and the escalating cost of keeping him another year, to answer the real question: is this player a bargain to hold, now and next season.

Offer grading. Takes a batch of real offers you have received and ranks them by next year's keeper surplus rather than this year's roster points, since a team out of contention gets almost nothing from winning a few more games this month.

Roster browser and a rebuild-targeted trade finder. Search a specific handful of contending teams for the shape of deal they would actually accept, rather than guessing.

A debugging story

One trade candidate's projection looked strong enough to anchor a real offer, until a line in the league's own constitution caught it: stats only count when a player is on a National League club's active roster. That player had actually become a real, unsigned free agent, no major-league team at all, but the projection source I was using had no way to know that and kept forecasting him for a full season off stale, pre-release data.

The fix was small once it was found: treat an unsigned player's rest-of-season line as zero until he signs somewhere, the same way the tool already had to handle injured players sitting on the 60-day IL. The numbers moved by a lot for something that took one conditional to fix.
